A classifier that outputs probabilities can be wrong in two different ways. It can put an email on the
wrong side of $0.5$, which accuracy measures. It can also attach the wrong number to an email, for
example saying $0.6$ when emails like it are spam $90\%$ of the time. The Bayes rule of
Section~\ref{sec:decision} compares $\post$ with $\thz=0.3$ and $\tho=0.97$, not with $0.5$, so it is the
second kind of error that drives the cost. This section measures the quality of the probabilities
themselves.

\paragraph{What we measure.}
On the test part of each split we compute four probability scores:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Log-loss}, $-\frac1N\sum_n\big[y_n\log\post_n + (1-y_n)\log(1-\post_n)\big]$. This is the
        cross-entropy error that logistic regression minimises, averaged per email. We clip $\post$ to
        $[10^{-12},\,1-10^{-12}]$, so that one confident mistake costs at most $27.6$ instead of infinity.
  \item \emph{Brier score}, $\frac1N\sum_n(\post_n-y_n)^2$: the squared distance between the probability and
        the $0/1$ label. It is $0$ for perfect predictions and $0.25$ for always saying $0.5$.
  \item \emph{Reliability diagram}: sort the predictions into $10$ bins and plot, for each bin, the average
        predicted $\post$ against the fraction of those emails that really are spam. A calibrated model puts
        every point on the diagonal. We use equal-width bins $[0,0.1),[0.1,0.2),\dots$ and equal-count bins
        (each holds a tenth of the emails, so no bin is nearly empty).
  \item \emph{Expected calibration error}, $\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_b \frac{n_b}{N}\,
        \big|\bar y_b - \bar\post_b\big|$: the gap between observed and predicted spam rates in each bin,
        weighted by the bin's share of the emails.
\end{itemize}
Next to these we report the accuracy of the labels ``spam iff $\post\ge0.5$'' and two quantities for the
Bayes rule with $\cfp=10$, $\cfn=1$, $\ci=0.3$: its realized loss per email and the fraction of emails it
inspects.

\paragraph{Setup.}
The \emph{synthetic} data are $4{,}601$ points (the size of Spambase) from two 2-D Gaussian classes with a
shared covariance, means $(0,0)$ and $(1.5,1)$, and $\pio=0.4$. Both models are correctly specified here,
so they should be calibrated. This is a check of the code, and the exact posterior gives a reference.
On \emph{Spambase} the features are transformed by $\log(1+x)$ and standardised (Section~1). The Gaussian
model uses one shared covariance. The covariance shrinkage of the Gaussian model and the L2 strength of
logistic regression are chosen on the validation part by \emph{log-loss}, because we want good
probabilities, not only good labels. All numbers are means over the 20 splits with standard errors
(Table~\ref{tab:c4-scores}).

\paragraph{Synthetic data.}
Both models score the same as the exact posterior: log-loss $0.433$ against $0.432$, Brier score
$0.139$ for all three, and the same cost. Their reliability curves lie on the diagonal
(Figure~\ref{fig:c4-rel}, top). One detail matters when reading ECE: even the exact posterior has
$\mathrm{ECE}\approx0.03$. Each split has only $919$ test points, so the observed spam fraction in each bin is
noisy, and ECE adds up absolute values of this noise. On data of this size, an ECE of about $0.03$ is the
noise floor and does not mean the model is miscalibrated.

\paragraph{Spambase.}
Logistic regression has better probabilities than the Gaussian model on every score:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item log-loss $0.186$ against $0.224$;
  \item Brier score $0.050$ against $0.061$;
  \item ECE $0.021$ against $0.039$.
\end{itemize}
It also has the lower cost, $0.124$ against $0.142$ per email. The Gaussian model's reliability curve lies
above the diagonal almost everywhere, most of all for $\post$ between $0.3$ and $0.6$
(Figure~\ref{fig:c4-rel}, bottom left). For example,
emails it gives about $0.45$ are spam about $65\%$ of the time, so it understates the spam probability in the
middle range. This is where the inspection threshold $\thz=0.3$ lies. The reason is the model's assumption
that $p(x\mid y)$ is Gaussian. Spambase features are word counts with many zeros, and even after
$\log(1+x)$ they are far from Gaussian. Logistic regression models $\Prob(y\mid x)$ directly and is trained
by minimising the log-loss itself, so it is fitted to give good probabilities. Note also that the two models
differ by only $1.3$ points of accuracy but by $15\%$ in cost.

\paragraph{Same accuracy, different cost (temperature scaling).}
To isolate the effect of the probabilities, we take the fitted logistic model on Spambase and change only how
confident it is:
\[
  \post_T = \sigm\big(\logit\post\,/\,T\big), \qquad T>0 .
\]
Dividing the log-odds by $T$ never changes their sign. So every email stays on the same side of $0.5$, and
the labels and the accuracy are \emph{exactly} the same for every $T$ (Table~\ref{tab:c4-temp},
Figure~\ref{fig:c4-temp}). $T<1$ pushes probabilities towards $0$ and $1$ (over-confident), and $T>1$ pulls
them towards $0.5$ (under-confident).

The decisions do change, because the Bayes rule compares $\post_T$ with $0.3$ and $0.97$. At $T=0.4$ many
emails are pushed above $0.97$ and go straight to the spam folder: inspection falls from $20.5\%$ to $7.3\%$,
real emails sent to spam rise from $3.8$ to $10.6$ per test split (at a cost of $10$ each), and the cost
rises from $0.124$ to $0.166$ ($+34\%$). At
$T=2.5$ the probabilities crowd around $0.5$, $46\%$ of all emails are inspected, and the cost rises to
$0.149$. Log-loss detects both changes ($0.290$ and $0.248$ against $0.186$). The Brier score hardly notices
over-confidence ($0.053$ against $0.050$): a confident mistake costs at most $1$ in squared error but
$-\log\post$ grows without bound. The lesson is that two models with identical accuracy can have very
different costs, and only a probability score can tell them apart.

\paragraph{Re-calibrating on validation data (Platt scaling).}
If a model's probabilities are off, we can repair them without refitting the model. We fit
\[
  \post' = \sigm\big(A\,\logit\post + B\big)
\]
on the \emph{validation} part. This is a logistic regression with the single feature $\logit\post$ plus an
intercept, so we fit it with the same Newton/IRLS update as in the lectures. $A$ plays the role of $1/T$,
$B$ shifts the probabilities up or down, and $A=1$, $B=0$ leaves $\post$ unchanged. For the Gaussian model on
Spambase this lowers ECE from $0.039$ to $0.024$, log-loss from $0.224$ to $0.208$, and the cost from $0.142$
to $0.132$. The logistic model was already close to calibrated: its log-loss hardly changes, and its cost
falls slightly, from $0.124$ to $0.118$. On the synthetic data, where both models are already calibrated,
Platt scaling changes nothing. Because $B$ moves the point where $\post'=0.5$, accuracy can change a little.

\begin{assumptions}
Test emails come from the same distribution as the training and validation emails (no prior shift; that
is Section~\ref{sec:prior-shift}). ECE depends on the number of bins and has a positive bias on small
test sets. Platt scaling assumes that a straight-line correction of the log-odds is enough.
\end{assumptions}

\emph{Code and checks.} \texttt{calibration.py} implements every score and both re-scalings.
The unit tests in \texttt{tests/test\_calibration.py} check the following:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item the log-loss on the worked example of the lecture notes;
  \item log-loss and Brier score against scikit-learn;
  \item the reliability bins on a small example worked by hand;
  \item that ECE is near $0$ for data that are calibrated by construction, and large after over-confident
        scaling;
  \item that on calibrated data no temperature beats $T=1$ for either score (both are proper scoring
        rules);
  \item that Platt scaling recovers a known $(A,B)$ and matches scikit-learn's logistic regression.
\end{itemize}
